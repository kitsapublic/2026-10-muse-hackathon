# Simulate Clinical Trials: protocol-stage eligibility optimization

The application supports the decision made while a protocol is being written: which scientifically justified eligibility criteria identify the largest appropriate patient population? Recruitment follows the finalized protocol. This demo evaluates eligibility; its measured outcome is the eligible population under each tested set of criteria.

## Five-minute walkthrough

1. **0:00–0:50 — Design the eligibility criteria.** “What if we could bring new medicines to patients in half the time? Discovery to market can take 10–15 years. A promising drug still needs to be tested in people, and a trial cannot finish without enough eligible participants. The decision about who can participate is made much earlier, while the protocol is written. This application lets medical writers test those criteria against EHR evidence before finalizing them.” Show the trial in focus: an open-label, single-arm study of ELX-101 in advanced NSCLC. The draft contains eight inclusion criteria and two exclusions. Explain the objective: maximize the eligible population while preserving scientific purpose and safety requirements.
2. **0:50–1:35 — Read the patient’s EHR.** “Assume these 100 synthetic patients represent our target population. In production, the intended workflow connects to EHR data across millions of patients. EHRs combine structured diagnoses, medications, vitals and labs with notes and treatment histories. Here is patient 011: age 78, stage IVA NSCLC, ECOG 0, acceptable blood counts, three prior systemic regimens. She meets the other eight criteria but fails the draft age and prior-treatment limits.” Show the dated treatment history and actual clinical notes. The Muse-powered application combines structured and unstructured EHR data: Muse extracts narrative evidence with citations, and the engine checks numbers and dates. Show the two-row before/after comparison: age 78 fails an upper limit of 75 but meets a limit of 80; three prior systemic regimens fail a maximum of two but meet a maximum of three. She qualifies only when both thresholds change.
3. **1:35–2:15 — Evaluate the draft criteria.** Run the recorded baseline: **19 eligible, 57 not eligible, 24 unresolved**. Show the cumulative waterfall: 100 patients narrow to 19 who meet every criterion. Red shows newly excluded patients at each step; amber tracks unresolved eligibility separately. Click a patient: each named criterion appears beside its full wording, the actual EHR observation or cited note, and the eligibility result. Explain inclusion versus exclusion explicitly: all inclusions must be met; no exclusion may apply.
4. **2:15–3:15 — Optimize eligibility criteria.** “The clinical team is evaluating two draft thresholds. Raising the maximum age from 75 to 80 gives **29 eligible**. Allowing three prior systemic regimens instead of two gives **23**. Applying both gives **37**.” The other eight criteria remain fixed. Open patient 011 to show draft versus revised criteria: she needs both changes. The patient population and underlying EHR evidence are the same in every comparison; only the selected eligibility parameters change.
5. **3:15–3:50 — Compare eligible populations.** The combined scenario adds **18 eligible patients**: 10 require the age change, four the prior-treatment change, and four both. That is **19 → 37 eligible patients in the same sample**. Show the draft and selected scenario waterfalls side by side, aligned by criterion and on the same scale. The age and prior-regimen rows highlight the changed thresholds; choosing a different scenario updates the right-hand waterfall. Unresolved eligibility changes from 24 to 30 because six patients who no longer fail a criterion still have an unanswered clinical question. This is the population impact the medical writer can take into protocol review. The original Muse run made 391 requests in 65.6 seconds for a calculated $0.6452; replay and threshold comparisons make no new model calls.
6. **3:50–4:30 — Review the proposed criteria.** “We now have the proposed thresholds, their population impact and the patient-level evidence. The clinical team can review the scientific rationale before finalizing the protocol. The goal is to write criteria that fit the population from the start, giving recruitment a stronger starting point and helping new treatments reach patients sooner.” Export the review package.

## Technical sections

Every step includes a short Technical note focused on that screen: rule compilation, citation extraction, cumulative evaluation, fact reuse, patient-level comparisons or review export. The notes replace the repeated inputs/workflow/Muse/outputs panels. Use them for technical judges or the final 30 seconds of the walkthrough:

1. **Protocol interpretation:** draft criterion text → Muse rule proposal → validation and human review → executable criteria. The benchmark uses reviewed reference rules; raw Muse proposals remain reviewable.
2. **EHR evidence reading:** a question plus notes within its observation window → Muse Spark 1.3 via Meta Model API → validated yes/no/undetermined answer with note citations. Structured values and dates go to the deterministic engine.
3. **Screening orchestration:** reviewed rules plus the cohort → durable narrative tasks and deterministic evaluation → a patient-by-criterion matrix and run metrics.
4. **Scenario optimization:** explicit threshold edits plus the same baseline facts → deterministic re-evaluation without new model calls → candidate populations. A production optimization agent would search within clinician-approved ranges.
5. **Comparison and evaluation:** aligned result matrices → patient-level transition analysis → population deltas and reasons. Historical independent scoring supplies model-performance measurements.
6. **Review handoff:** selected criteria, results and evidence → a version-bound JSON review package with report/CSV links → clinical review. A production writing agent could draft the approved protocol revision for human approval.

The opening places the problem, approach and goal immediately below the compact ambition statement. There is no timeline graph. The guided walkthrough has six steps and no site-selection requirement.

## Presenter reference

- The opening's “half the time” is the ambition, not a measured result. [U.S. GAO](https://www.gao.gov/blog/could-ai-help-create-new-medicines) gives the 10–15-year discovery-to-market timeline. [ASCO's eligibility guidance](https://www.asco.org/research-data/clinical-trials/clinical-trial-eligibility-criteria) supports reviewing unnecessarily restrictive criteria.
- This is an invented protocol and synthetic population. Representativeness is an assumption for the demonstration, not a measured property of the sample. No clinical benefit or reduction in development time has been measured.
- The tested alternatives compare age and prior systemic regimen thresholds; the demo does not automatically establish clinical appropriateness or a global optimum.
- All model answers in recorded mode are retained Muse responses. The benchmark uses reviewed reference interpretations of the protocol. Original pending Muse criterion proposals and execution details remain in the inspector.
- Eligibility changes after approval require the appropriate protocol amendment process; the product's point is to inform those decisions during authoring, before launch.
